What Is UIL Copy Editing?

Contest Format

  • Time limit: 15 minutes
  • Part 1 (15 pts): 15 questions — pick the correct option from inline choices in a sentence
  • Part 2 (20 pts): 5 sentences with exactly 4 errors each (4 pts per sentence) — use editing marks or rewrite
  • Part 3 (tiebreaker): Edit a news brief for all errors — required but used only to break ties
  • Total: 35 points (Part 1 + Part 2)
  • Allowed resources: AP Stylebook, dictionary, thesaurus
Strategy: Part 1 is the fastest way to earn points — each question is worth 1 point and can be answered quickly. Spend about 4-5 minutes on Part 1, 6-7 minutes on Part 2, and the remaining time on Part 3. Never skip Part 3 or your entry may be disqualified!

Most-Tested AP Style Rules

Numbers

  • Spell out one through nine; use figures for 10 and above
  • Always use figures for ages, percents, dollars, addresses, dates, scores
  • Spell out a number at the start of a sentence (or rephrase)
  • Use percent (one word), not % in regular text
She has 3 cats and 15 dogs.
She has three cats and 15 dogs.

The girl is nine years old.
The girl is 9 years old. (ages always use figures)

Capitalization

  • Titles before names: capitalize. After or alone: lowercase.
    President Smith vs. Smith, the president
  • Seasons: always lowercase (spring, fall) unless part of a proper name
  • Directions: lowercase when compass direction (drove north), capitalize when region (the South)
  • School subjects: lowercase unless a language or proper noun (math, English, history)
The Mayor spoke at the Fall festival.
The mayor spoke at the fall festival.

She teaches History and english.
She teaches history and English.

Abbreviations

  • States: Abbreviate after a city name using AP abbreviations (not postal codes): Austin, Texas — never Austin, TX
  • Months: Abbreviate months with 6+ letters when used with a specific date: Feb. 14 but February 2025
  • Never abbreviate: March, April, May, June, July
  • a.m. / p.m. — lowercase with periods. 10 a.m. (not AM or A.M.)
  • Titles: Abbreviate Mr., Mrs., Dr., Gov., Rep., Sen. before a full name
The meeting is at 3 PM on January 5.
The meeting is at 3 p.m. on Jan. 5.

He moved to Houston, TX in May.
He moved to Houston, Texas, in May. (state name after city gets commas around it)

Commas

  • No Oxford comma in AP style: red, white and blue
  • City-state: Comma after city AND after state: Dallas, Texas, is big.
  • Ages as appositives: Commas around age: John Smith, 42, said...
  • Introductory clauses: Comma after intro clause: After the game, the team celebrated.
  • Compound sentences: Comma before the conjunction: She ran, and he walked.
She bought apples, oranges, and bananas.
She bought apples, oranges and bananas. (no Oxford comma)

John Smith 42 of Dallas Texas said he was happy.
John Smith, 42, of Dallas, Texas, said he was happy.

Commonly Confused Words

  • affect (verb) vs. effect (noun)
  • its (possessive) vs. it's (it is)
  • their/there/they're
  • who (subject) vs. whom (object)
  • that (restrictive, no comma) vs. which (non-restrictive, use commas)
  • fewer (countable) vs. less (uncountable)
  • farther (physical distance) vs. further (figurative)
The team, that won the game, celebrated.
The team that won the game celebrated. (restrictive — no commas)

Less than 10 students showed up.
Fewer than 10 students showed up. (countable)

Punctuation

  • Quotation marks: Periods and commas go INSIDE. Colons and semicolons go OUTSIDE.
  • Possessives: Singular: add 's (even for names ending in s: James's). Plural: add s'
  • Hyphens: Use hyphens in compound modifiers before a noun: well-known author but the author is well known
  • Semicolons: Join two independent clauses without a conjunction

Other Frequently Tested Rules

  • Datelines: CITY NAME in caps, state or country: DALLAS, Texas —
  • Courtesy titles: Generally not used on first reference in news stories. Use full name first reference, last name only on subsequent references.
  • Said: Use said for attribution — avoid stated, exclaimed, expressed
  • Days/dates: Don't use last or next — use the actual day or date
  • Compositions: Book titles, movie titles, etc. — use quotation marks (AP does not use italics)

Standard Editing Marks

On the real UIL test, Part 2 uses editing marks. Here are the standard marks you should know:

MarkMeaningExample
=Capitalizedallas → Dallas (draw triple underline under letter)
/LowercaseThe Mayor → The mayor (slash through capital letter)
⌃InsertInsert a missing word, letter, comma, or punctuation (caret mark)
⌒DeleteRemove a word or letter (loop around it)
⊙Insert periodCircle where a period should go

⤺TransposeSwap the order of two adjacent words or letters

Part 2 tip: On the real test, you use editing marks directly on the printed sentences. In this digital trainer, you rewrite the corrected sentence instead. The skills are the same — spotting the 4 errors is what matters!

Contest Strategy

Time Management (15 minutes total)

  • Part 1 (4-5 min): These are fast. Read each sentence, pick the correct option. If you're stuck, skip it and come back.
  • Part 2 (6-7 min): Read each sentence slowly. Look for exactly 4 errors. Common categories: spelling, AP style, grammar, punctuation.
  • Part 3 (3-4 min): Don't overthink it — correct everything you can see. This is only used for tiebreakers, but you MUST attempt it.

What to Look For (Error Checklist)

  • Spelling: Commonly misspelled words (accommodate, occurrence, separate, cemetery, etc.)
  • AP numbers: One-nine spelled out? 10+ as figures? Ages/dates/money as figures?
  • Capitalization: Titles, seasons, directions, school subjects
  • Commas: Oxford comma removed? City/state commas? Age appositives?
  • Abbreviations: State names, months, a.m./p.m., titles
  • Word choice: affect/effect, its/it's, fewer/less, that/which
  • Subject-verb agreement: Collective nouns (team, committee) take singular verbs
  • Punctuation: Quotation marks placement, possessives, hyphens
Pro tip: In Part 2, the judges only look at your first 4 corrections. If you mark more than 4, only the first 4 count. So be confident in your corrections — quality over quantity. A compound error (like a misspelled word that's also miscapitalized) counts as one error.

Study Resources

How to Prepare

  • AP Stylebook: Read the entries for numbers, abbreviations, capitalization, and punctuation first
  • Practice tests: Use the trainer to run timed mock contests regularly
  • Skill drills: Target specific skills like AP numbers, commas, or word choice
  • Read newspaper articles: Train your eye by reading professionally edited copy
  • Study commonly misspelled words: Keep a personal list of words you miss
