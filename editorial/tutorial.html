What Is UIL Editorial Writing?

Contest Format

  • Time limit: 45 minutes
  • What you receive: A fact sheet about a school policy issue at Leaguetown High School
  • What you write: A staff editorial for the school newspaper (the Leaguetown Press)
  • Your goal: Take a clear stance FOR or AGAINST the proposal and persuade readers
  • Length: 300-450 words (no more than 450)
  • Allowed resources: AP Stylebook, dictionary, thesaurus
  • Judging: At least 3 judges rank the top 6 papers. No ties allowed.
Key principle: An editorial supports or opposes a policy or action. It does NOT sermonize. Your job is to make thoughtful, logical arguments — not to lecture or moralize.

The Editorial Formula

UIL editorials follow a clear structure. Here are the building blocks:

1

Lead — State the Situation

Open with 1-2 sentences summarizing the issue. What's happening? What's at stake? Get right to the point — don't waddle into it.

2

Stance — Take a Clear Position

Within the first 2-3 paragraphs, state your position clearly and definitively. "The school board should reject the proposal." No ambiguity.

3

Body — Support Your Stance (2-3 reasons)

Give 2-3 strong reasons supporting your position. Use specific facts, numbers, and details from the fact sheet. Each reason should be its own paragraph.

4

Rebuttal — Address the Opposition

Acknowledge the other side's strongest argument, then explain why your position is still better. Use specific examples to refute them.

5

Solution — Offer a Specific Action

What should happen next? Be specific. "The board should vote against the proposal" or "The administration should form a student committee to..."

6

Conclusion — Recap the Stance

End with a strong final sentence that reinforces your position. Leave the reader with no doubt about where you stand.

Do's and Don'ts

DO

  • Get right to the point
  • Take a clear, definitive stance early
  • Use first person plural: "we" (= the newspaper staff)
  • Use specific facts and numbers from the fact sheet
  • Refute the opposition with evidence
  • Offer a specific solution or call to action
  • Use the future angle (your paper comes out BEFORE the vote)
  • Paraphrase information rather than using direct quotes
  • Stay under 450 words

DON'T

  • Use first person singular: "I, me, mine"
  • Use second person: "you, your"
  • Ask questions — editorials answer questions
  • Sermonize or lecture the reader
  • Use direct quotes from the fact sheet
  • Be wishy-washy or take both sides
  • Write more than 450 words
  • Start with "In my opinion..." or "I think..."
  • Make factual errors (check names, numbers, dates)

Finding Your Voice

Pronoun Rules

  • Best approach: Avoid pronouns entirely. Write "The district should..." instead of "We believe the district should..."
  • If you must use a pronoun: Use first person plural — we, us, our (meaning "we, the newspaper staff")
  • Never use: I, me, my, mine (first person singular) or you, your (second person)
Bad — uses "I" and "you"
"I think the school board should reject this proposal. You should care about this because it affects your education."
Good — uses third person and "we" (staff)
"The school board should reject this proposal. Students deserve a quality education, and this policy threatens that."

Writing a Strong Lead

The lead is the most important part. Judges form their first impression in the opening paragraph. State the situation quickly and take your stance early.
Weak lead — too vague, no stance
"There has been a lot of discussion about a new policy at our school. Some people think it's good and some people think it's bad. This is a complicated issue."
Strong lead — situation + stance
"Eight seniors face three days of out-of-school suspension after staging a peaceful walkout to raise awareness about climate change. A disciplinary committee will vote on their appeal Tuesday. The committee should overturn the suspension and allow the seniors to attend their last days of school."

Using Information from the Fact Sheet

How to Use Facts Effectively

  • Paraphrase, don't quote: Pull information from the quotes but put it in your own words. Avoid direct quotes unless the source has substantial credibility.
  • Use specific numbers: Instead of "many students," write "the school's 1,430 students"
  • Reference the timeline: Mention when the vote/meeting happens and when your paper comes out
  • Attribution is optional: You don't need to say "According to the superintendent..." — just state the fact
  • Check for accuracy: Double-check names, numbers, and dates from the fact sheet. Factual errors hurt your ranking.

Time Management (45 minutes)

Suggested Breakdown

  • Minutes 1-8: Read the fact sheet carefully. Highlight key facts. Decide your stance.
  • Minutes 8-12: Outline your editorial (stance, 2-3 reasons, rebuttal, solution).
  • Minutes 12-38: Write your editorial. Follow your outline.
  • Minutes 38-45: Proofread for grammar, AP style, factual errors, and word count.
Pro tip: If you finish early, use the remaining time to proofread — not to add more content. Clean, concise writing scores higher than long, rambling writing. The best editorials are often 350-400 words.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Why Editorials Lose Points

  • No clear stance: The #1 mistake. Judges need to know your position immediately.
  • Sermonizing: "Students need to understand that education is important and they should value it." This is lecturing, not arguing.
  • No rebuttal: Ignoring the opposition makes your argument look one-sided.
  • No solution: Saying "something should be done" isn't enough. Be specific.
  • Using "I": This is a staff editorial, not a personal essay.
  • Asking questions: "Shouldn't we care about our students?" — Editorials answer questions, not ask them.
  • Too long: Going over 450 words suggests poor editing skills.
  • Factual errors: Getting a name, number, or date wrong from the fact sheet is a serious mistake.
  • No future angle: Your paper comes out BEFORE the vote. Use that. "The board should vote tomorrow to..."
