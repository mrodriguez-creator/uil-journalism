1. What Is UIL News Writing?

In the UIL News Writing contest, you receive a fact sheet containing background information and source quotes about a school event. Your job is to write a news story in inverted pyramid format as if you were a reporter for the school newspaper.

  • Time: 45 minutes
  • Format: Inverted pyramid (most important info first)
  • Setting: You are a reporter for the "Leaguetown Press"
  • Goal: Write a clear, objective, well-organized news story
Key insight: You will have MORE information than you need. Good news judgment means choosing what matters most and leaving out the rest.

2. Writing a Strong Lead

Your lead (first paragraph) is the most important part of your story. It should answer the key W's: Who, What, When, Where, Why/How. Aim for 25-35 words.

The #1 Rule: Lead with What's Coming

The fact sheet will tell you when the paper is being published (e.g., "You are writing for the issue to be distributed Jan. 7"). Your lead should focus on the upcoming event — the vote, meeting, or decision that happens AFTER the paper comes out.

✅ Strong Lead (future-focused)
The district could begin serving free dinner for students at school if the school board approves Superintendent Mark Rothstein's proposal at its meeting Thursday.
❌ Weak Lead (past-focused)
In March, the district announced that it anticipated an $18 million budget shortfall ahead of the 2025-2026 school year.

Lead Tips

  • Start with What or Who — not When or Where
  • Focus on the future — what will happen, not just what has happened
  • Be specific — include key details (dollar amounts, vote dates, etc.)
  • Keep it concise — 25-35 words, one sentence
  • Don't start with "On Monday..." or "At Leaguetown High School..."

3. The LQTQ Formula

After your lead, follow the LQTQ formula: Lead, Quote, Transition, Quote. This creates a rhythm that judges love.

1
LEAD — Most timely, newsworthy information. Answer the 5 W's. Focus on what's coming up.
2
ADDITIONAL INFO — Important facts NOT in the lead. Set up context for the first quote.
3
DIRECT QUOTE #1 — From the most important source. Should connect to the info above. Attribution after the first sentence.
4
TRANSITION — A new fact, indirect quote, or context that bridges to the next quote. Provides information that keeps the story flowing.
5
DIRECT QUOTE #2 — From a different source. Connects to the transition above.
6
REPEAT — Continue the transition/quote pattern with remaining sources. End with a strong closing quote.
Never stack quotes! Two quotes from different people should NEVER appear back-to-back without a transition between them.

4. Quotes and Attribution

Attribution Format

Always use noun then verb: "Name said" — never "said Name."

✅ Correct Attribution
"Class sizes will likely be a bit larger," Superintendent Leslie Bruck said.
"This is devastating for our kids," parent Gregory Alan said.
❌ Wrong Attribution
"Class sizes will likely be a bit larger," said Superintendent Leslie Bruck.
"This is devastating," said Gregory Alan, a parent.

Quote Rules

  • Use "said" — not "exclaimed," "stated," "expressed," "noted"
  • First mention: Full name + title ("Superintendent Leslie Bruck said")
  • Later mentions: Last name only for adults ("Bruck said"), first name or pronoun for students ("she said")
  • Place attribution after the first sentence in multi-sentence quotes
  • Each direct quote gets its own paragraph
  • Don't repeat the transition in the quote — the quote should add new information

Direct vs. Indirect Quotes

Direct quotes use exact words in quotation marks. Indirect quotes paraphrase without quotation marks. Use both to vary your writing.

Indirect quote (paraphrase)
Junior Hannah Hillier said since her dad works late shifts, she is responsible for providing dinner for her siblings.
Followed by a direct quote
"If I didn't have to worry about making dinner for my brother and sister, I would be able to join Art Club or have more time to do my homework," she said.

5. Inverted Pyramid Structure

The inverted pyramid means organizing information from most important to least important. A well-structured story can be cut from the bottom at any point without losing essential meaning.

Story Structure (Top to Bottom)

LEAD — Most important facts (Who, What, When, Where, Why)
KEY DETAILS — Additional important context and background
SUPPORTING INFO — Quotes, reactions, related facts
BACKGROUND — Less essential details, closing quote

Bobby Hawthorne's Checklist (Official UIL)

  1. Does the story tell the most important facts in order of importance?
  2. Is information attributed to the correct source?
  3. Are paragraphs short, averaging 25-35 words?
  4. Is the story free of editorial comment and first-person pronouns?
  5. Do paragraphs follow one another in logical order?
  6. Is sentence structure varied?
  7. Could the last paragraph or two be cut without damaging the story?
  8. Does the vocabulary catch the spirit of the story?
  9. Is the story concise? Can any words be eliminated?
  10. Does the story have unity — no unnecessary or irrelevant details?

6. Do's and Don'ts

✅ DO

  • Write in third person throughout
  • Use "said" for all attribution
  • Keep paragraphs to 1-2 sentences
  • Lead with the most timely angle
  • Use the LQTQ formula
  • Spell all names correctly from the fact sheet
  • Follow AP style for numbers, dates, titles
  • End with a strong closing quote
  • Include transitions between quotes
  • Use both direct and indirect quotes

❌ DON'T

  • Use first person (I, we, our) or second person (you)
  • Editorialize or include personal opinions
  • Stack two quotes without a transition
  • Use "said Name" instead of "Name said"
  • Start with "On Monday..." or "At Leaguetown..."
  • Write long paragraphs (3+ sentences)
  • Repeat information from the transition in the quote
  • Use ALL information — choose what matters
  • Misspell names from the fact sheet
  • Use "exclaimed," "stated," or other attribution verbs

7. AP Style Quick Reference

Numbers

  • Spell out one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above
  • Always use figures for: ages, scores, prices, percentages, times, addresses
  • Use "noon" and "midnight" — not "12 p.m." or "12 a.m."
  • No leading zeros in dollar amounts: $5, not $5.00

Dates & Times

  • Abbreviate months with dates: Jan. 7, Feb. 14, Aug. 5
  • Don't abbreviate: March, April, May, June, July
  • Use lowercase a.m. and p.m. with periods
  • Use the day of the week if within 7 days: "Thursday" not "Jan. 9"

Titles

  • Capitalize titles before names: "Superintendent Leslie Bruck"
  • Lowercase titles after names: "Leslie Bruck, superintendent"
  • Lowercase grade levels: freshman, sophomore, junior, senior

Punctuation

  • No Oxford comma: "red, white and blue" (no comma before "and")
  • Commas and periods go inside quotation marks
  • Use a comma before attribution: "This is great," she said.

8. Time Management (45 Minutes)

TimeMinutesWhat to Do
0:00 - 5:005 minRead the entire fact sheet once for overall understanding. Identify the most newsworthy angle.
5:00 - 10:005 minRe-read and mark up. Highlight key facts, circle the best quotes, note names/titles. Identify your lead angle.
10:00 - 12:002 minPlan your story order. Number the quotes in the order you'll use them. Pick 3-4 sources.
12:00 - 38:0026 minWrite your story. Start with the lead, then follow the LQTQ formula. Don't worry about perfection — get it down.
38:00 - 45:007 minProofread carefully. Check: spelling of names, AP style, attribution format, any editorializing, paragraph length, grammar.
Pro tip: Spend extra time on your lead. A strong lead sets up the entire story and is the first thing judges evaluate. If your lead is weak, the rest of the story suffers.

9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Burying the lead — Starting with background instead of the most timely angle
  2. Stacking quotes — Putting two quotes from different people back-to-back
  3. "Said Name" — Using "said Bruck" instead of "Bruck said"
  4. Editorializing — Adding opinions like "unfortunately" or "this is a great idea"
  5. Using first/second person — Writing "our school," "we," or "you" outside of quotes
  6. Misspelling names — Copy names exactly from the fact sheet
  7. Long paragraphs — News paragraphs should be 1-2 sentences max
  8. Repeating transition info in quotes — The quote should add NEW information
  9. Using all the information — Not everything needs to be in your story
  10. Weak closing — End with a strong, forward-looking quote, not background facts
Disqualification risks: Writing your name or school name on the entry. Using second person ("you") extensively in your story. Any of these can result in disqualification.

10. Sample Story Structure

Here's the general structure of a well-organized news story, based on real UIL winning entries:

Paragraph 1: LEAD
[Summary lead — most timely angle, 25-35 words, future-focused]
Paragraph 2: ADDITIONAL CONTEXT
[1-2 sentences expanding on the lead with key background facts]
Paragraph 3: QUOTE #1 (most important source)
"First sentence of quote," Title Name said. "Second sentence of quote. Maybe a third."
Paragraph 4: TRANSITION
[New fact or context that sets up the next quote]
Paragraph 5: QUOTE #2 (different source)
"Quote that connects to the transition above," Name said.
Paragraph 6: TRANSITION
[More context, indirect quote, or a partial quote bridging to next source]
Paragraph 7: QUOTE #3
"A third perspective on the issue," Name said.
Paragraph 8+: Continue pattern...
[Additional transitions and quotes as needed]
Final Paragraph: STRONG CLOSING QUOTE
"A memorable, forward-looking quote that wraps up the story," Name said.
